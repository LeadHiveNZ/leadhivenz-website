Downpipes · Whanganui & beyond

Downpipes that put the water where it belongs.

Spouting collects the rain. Downpipes decide where it ends up. Dylan fits, replaces and re-routes downpipes and rainwater heads across Whanganui, Manawatū, Rangitīkei and South Taranaki.

01Why they matter

Where the water goes is the whole point.

Every litre of rain that lands on your roof has to leave through a downpipe. If the downpipes are too few, blocked, cracked or emptying in the wrong place, the water ends up somewhere you don't want it.

What goes wrong

  • Water at the foundations. It softens the ground beside the house and can find its way under the floor.
  • Splashback on the walls. Green staining first, then rot at the base of weatherboards and cladding.
  • Overflow at the spouting. When a downpipe can't keep up, water spills over the front and runs down the wall.
  • Eroded gardens and soggy paths. Puddles by the front door, and bare patches where the water lands.

None of this is dramatic on the day. It shows up a winter or two later, when the damage is already done. Good downpipes are cheap insurance against all of it.

02How many you need

Enough downpipes, in the right places.

There's no magic number. Dylan looks at your roof and your runs and works out what suits the house.

  • How much roof drains to each outletThe more roof that feeds one downpipe, the more water it has to carry in a downpour. Bigger roofs need more downpipes than small ones.
  • How long the runs areA long, straight run of spouting usually needs an outlet partway along, not just one at the far end.
  • The fall of the spoutingSpouting slopes gently towards each outlet. We plan the downpipe positions and the fall together, so water never has far to travel.
  • Pitch and exposureA steep roof sheds water fast. A valley feeding one corner, or an exposed site, concentrates it. Both call for more capacity.
  • Size and shapeDownpipes come in round and rectangular sections and in different sizes. We match them to your spouting profile and outlets so the flow isn't choked at the join.
  • Where the water ends upA stormwater drain, a soakage pit, a tank, or the ground well clear of the house. Council rules vary, and a drain connection can need a drainlayer. We'll tell you what we see.

Rules of thumb only go so far. When we quote, we count the outlets and downpipes on your actual roof and list them, so you know why each one is there.

03Rainwater heads & box gutters

Heads for the big flows, and the tricky corners.

A rainwater head is the open box that sits at the top of a downpipe. The spouting or box gutter discharges into it, and the downpipe carries on from the bottom.

Because it is open, a head takes a large flow without choking. It also makes a blockage obvious: if the downpipe ever clogs, the water shows up outside, not hidden behind a wall or in a ceiling.

Heads also give a tidy, finished look at the eave, which is why you'll see them on character villas as well as sheds and commercial buildings.

We'll show the options and finishes on your quote. If your home has internal or box gutters, see our spouting replacement page.

A head suits

Where a plain outlet isn't enough

  • Box gutters and internal gutters on villas and older buildings
  • Valleys and junctions where two roof planes feed one point
  • Large or low-pitch roofs, including sheds and commercial buildings
  • High-flow spots that overflow in heavy rain
  • Corners where a downpipe has to start from a tight space

04Offsets, routing & materials

Run neatly down the wall, matched to the house.

A downpipe is on show all day. It should look as though it belongs.

The eave overhangs the wall, so a downpipe has to step in before it runs straight down. That step is the offset. Done well, it is a tight, tidy bend that sits close to the wall. Done badly, it is a lump that traps leaves and leaks at the joins.

Where we can, we run downpipes down corners, beside windows and along the lines the house already has, so they are the least noticeable thing on the wall. Brackets go in at proper spacing so the pipe doesn't lean, rattle or work loose.

05Rainwater tanks

Tanks and diverters: catch it, don't waste it.

Rural homes rely on tanks, and plenty of town gardens have one now. Getting water into the tank starts at the downpipe.

  • DivertersA diverter fits into the downpipe and sends water to the tank. When the tank is full, the water carries on down the pipe to the drain, so nothing spills where it shouldn't.
  • First flushA first-flush diverter holds back the first dirty water off the roof, such as dust, bird droppings and leaf bits, before the tank fills. Your tank stays cleaner.
  • Rural and lifestyle propertiesSheds, barns and houses on larger blocks can have long distances between roof and tank. We plan the run so water reaches the tank and the overflow lands well clear of buildings and yards.
  • Garden tanks in townA smaller tank beside the garage or shed needs a neat connection off the downpipe. The tank base, any pump and house plumbing are separate jobs, so tell us what you have.

Drinking from your tank? The roof, the spouting, a first-flush diverter and filtration all matter. Ask a plumber or your council about treatment. We look after the roof end of the system, not the water treatment.

06Rusted or cracked downpipes

Replace the run, or just the bad length.

A loose bracket or a cracked bottom length, the part that gets knocked, can be swapped without touching the rest. If the pipe is rusting along its seams, split down its length or dented flat, replace the whole run.

It's also worth asking about downpipes whenever the spouting is being replaced. New spouting discharging into an old, tired pipe is a false economy, and doing both at once saves a second visit.

Signs it's time

What to look for

  • Rust bleeding from seams and joins
  • Splits or cracks, especially near the ground
  • Sections hanging loose or pulling away from the wall
  • Water running down the wall instead of inside the pipe
  • A base that empties against the foundation, or nowhere at all

How many downpipes does my house need?

It depends on how much roof drains to each outlet, how long the spouting runs are and the pitch of the roof. A larger or more complex roof needs more downpipes than a small, simple one. Dylan counts the outlets on your actual roof and lists them on the quote, so you can see exactly what you are paying for.

What is a rainwater head, and do I need one?

A rainwater head is an open box at the top of a downpipe. Spouting or a box gutter discharges into it. It copes with bigger flows, and if the downpipe ever blocks, the water shows itself outside instead of backing up somewhere worse. Heads suit box gutters, valleys and large roofs. Most standard spouting runs don't need one.

Can you match the downpipes to my spouting colour?

Yes. We match downpipes to your spouting by default, or to your fascia or walls if you prefer. COLORSTEEL® and ColorCote® use different colour names, so we confirm the exact match when we quote. Colours on a screen are only approximate, so check a physical sample or colour chart before you decide.

Can you connect a downpipe to my rainwater tank?

Yes. We fit diverters, including first-flush diverters, and connect your downpipes to the tank. The tank itself, its stand, any pump and the plumbing into the house are separate jobs, so tell us what you have when you call or send photos.

Can you replace just one downpipe?

Yes. A single rusted or cracked downpipe can be replaced on its own. If your spouting is also old, it is worth asking whether to do both together, and Dylan will tell you honestly which makes sense. You can send photos for a free quote.
