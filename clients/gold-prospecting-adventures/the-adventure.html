The gold panning adventure

A few days on the rivers around Murchison, learning to pan with a local who knows where the colour is.

Recreational gold prospecting

Real rivers, real gravel, real gold

This is gold panning the way the old-timers did it, minus the hardship. Ian takes you out into the rivers of Buller gold country, shows you how to read the water and work a pan, and lets you get stuck in.

There is no script and no conveyor belt. Days run at your pace, around the weather and the rivers, and you head home with new skills, a few stories and, with a bit of luck, some colour of your own.

Morning to lamplight

What a day looks like

Every day is a little different, depending on the weather and the water. Here is the general idea.

  1. Morning

    Breakfast and a game plan

    Start the day at Ian’s place in Murchison. Have a look at the river levels, sort out your gear, and pick a spot with the best chance of colour.

  2. On the river

    Learn the pan, then work the gravels

    Ian shows you how gold settles, where it hides in a river, and how to work a pan so the heavy stuff stays put. Then it is over to you: dig, pan, repeat, and look for that first glint.

  3. Afternoon

    Try another spot

    Move on to another bend or bar, try a different technique, or simply enjoy the quiet of the bush and the sound of the river.

  4. Evening

    Back to the homestead

    Head home, compare your finds, and swap gold-rush yarns as the light goes down over the hills.

A bit of technique

How gold panning works

Gold is roughly 19 times heavier than water, so with the right moves it sinks to the bottom of the pan while the lighter gravel washes away.

  1. Fill the pan

    Scoop up gravel from where gold is likely to settle: behind boulders, on the inside of bends, down near bedrock.

  2. Shake it down

    Under the water, shake the pan hard from side to side so the heavy material sinks to the bottom.

  3. Wash it off

    Tilt and swirl, letting the water carry the lighter stones and sand over the lip of the pan, a little at a time.

  4. Look for the colour

    What is left is heavy black sand, and with luck a glint of gold. Pick out the flakes and pop them in a vial.

A bit of history

Panning where the diggers did

Gold was found in the Buller and Lyell districts in the 1860s, and diggers made their way up the gorge on foot, on horseback and later by coach. Many worked the rivers with nothing more than a pan, a shovel and a sluice box.

Some of the techniques Ian will show you are much the same as theirs. Standing in the river with a pan in your hands, it is not hard to picture the gorge road a century ago, and the people who came looking for their fortune.
